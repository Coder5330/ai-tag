How this works

Each agent has its own 4‑layer network: 99 inputs → 64 → 64 → four action heads and a value estimate. The inputs are its own position, height, heading and velocity, whether it is on the ground and whether it is carrying something, all of the same for its opponent, where the crates are, the time left in the round, its previous action, and 13 raycasts that report the distance to whatever they hit and whether it was a wall, a crate or the other agent.

Rewards. When Kai tags Albert, Kai gets +1 and Albert gets −1. On top of that Albert earns +0.001 every frame he stays alive and Kai pays −0.001, so Kai is pushed to finish quickly rather than stall. A small potential‑based distance term is added to get the first chase started; because it telescopes it cannot change which strategy is optimal, only how fast it is found.

Whoever is losing gets trained. Chasing is far easier to learn than evading, so on a fixed schedule Kai compounds a lead until Albert has no winnable rounds left, his advantages go flat, and he stops learning altogether — measured, Albert decays from 58% escapes to 0% over a few hundred updates. So the trainer checks the real head‑to‑head score every few updates and hands the training turn to whichever agent is behind. Albert also starts from a supervised copy of a hand‑written evader, because PPO reliably fails to rediscover plain fleeing on its own.

Self‑play. Training both brains at once tends to collapse into a single brittle strategy, so only one trains at a time. Its opponent is drawn from a pool of the 10 most recent frozen copies, re‑drawn every round, so the trainee has to beat a spread of past strategies instead of overfitting to the newest one.

The room is 3D. Both agents jump under gravity, stand on top of the fixed blocks and the loose crates, and can grab a crate to carry, shove or throw it. A tag only counts when the two bodies overlap in height as well as on the floor, so timing a jump over an incoming Kai is a legal escape — and one Albert has to discover for himself. Hit the back wall hard enough, or throw a crate at it, and the scoreboards shatter; that part is purely for show and never touches the simulation.

Kai is faster in a straight line but turns in a wide 5.7‑unit arc; Albert is slower and turns on a 2.2‑unit one. So Albert's winning move is to make Kai commit and then cut across him while he is still swinging around.

Why rounds are short. In a closed room a pursuer corners an evader eventually whatever the speeds — a scripted evader escapes 0% of 10‑second rounds even when it is faster than the pursuer. The clock, not the physics, is what decides whether running away can ever pay, so a round lasts 6 seconds. Albert doesn't have to escape forever, just outlast the buzzer.

Inspired by AI Warehouse's AI Learns to Play Tag. Same recipe — deep RL, self‑play, raycast vision — shrunk down so it converges in minutes instead of days.
